Understanding & Treating Insomnia

Insomnia is one of the most common conditions we treat — and one of the most treatable. This guide covers everything you need to know about your diagnosis, the proven behavioral treatments that work better than medication long-term, your medication options, and how to get the most out of care at Buffington Family Medicine.

Program Overview

Insomnia is a condition where you have persistent difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early — and that difficulty affects how you feel or function during the day. It is not just stress and it is not something you have to live with. Effective, evidence-based treatments exist.

Most important things to know

  • CBT-I is the best long-term treatment — behavioral therapy outperforms medication for chronic insomnia and the results last. Medication is a useful bridge.
  • Sleep hygiene alone is rarely enough for true chronic insomnia, but it is always the foundation
  • Medication works quickly — most sleep medications help the first night you take them
  • Follow up in 2–4 weeks after starting a new treatment so we can assess your response
  • Contact our office at (817) 431-9199 if you experience severe side effects or your sleep does not improve
When to seek help right away

Call (817) 431-9199 or go to the ER if insomnia is accompanied by chest pain, thoughts of self-harm, inability to function safely at work or while driving, or severe anxiety or panic attacks.

What we offer

  • Thorough evaluation — we identify the type and cause of your insomnia before recommending treatment
  • CBT-I guidance — we walk you through the most effective behavioral treatment for insomnia, proven to work better than medication in the long run
  • Medication management — when medication is appropriate, we choose the right option for your situation and monitor closely
  • Comorbidity management — insomnia is often connected to anxiety, depression, ADHD, or chronic pain; we treat the whole picture
  • Sleep study referrals — when we suspect sleep apnea or another sleep disorder, we refer appropriately
You are not imagining it

Chronic insomnia is a real medical condition — not a character flaw, not a sign of weakness, and not something you should just push through. The consequences of untreated insomnia are significant and well-documented. We take sleep seriously at Buffington Family Medicine because we know how much it affects everything else.

Start Here: Your First Steps

If you only read one thing

Start with sleep hygiene tonight — consistent wake time, no screens before bed, get out of bed if you are awake more than 20 minutes. If you have medication, take it as directed. Schedule a follow-up in 2–4 weeks so we can review your response and adjust the plan.

If you were just prescribed sleep medication

  1. Take it as directed — only at bedtime, not during the night unless specifically prescribed for middle-of-the-night waking (e.g., Sonata / zaleplon)
  2. Do not drive or operate heavy machinery until you know how it affects you — most sleep medications cause next-day drowsiness initially
  3. Do not combine with alcohol — serious interaction risk
  4. Start sleep hygiene at the same time — medication works faster; sleep hygiene builds lasting change. Do both.
  5. Schedule your follow-up in 2–4 weeks so we can assess your response
  6. Call us at (817) 431-9199 if you experience unusual sleep behaviors, severe next-day sedation, or any concerning side effects

If you are starting behavioral treatment (CBT-I)

  1. Pick a fixed wake time and commit to it — even on weekends. This is the single most powerful sleep intervention.
  2. Get out of bed if you cannot sleep — after 20 minutes of wakefulness, go to another room and do something quiet until you feel sleepy again
  3. Use your bed only for sleep and sex — no phones, TV, work, or reading in bed
  4. Keep a sleep log — note your bedtime, estimated sleep time, awakenings, and wake time each morning. Even rough estimates are useful.
  5. Expect a rough first week — sleep restriction (a core CBT-I technique) gets worse before it gets better. This is normal and expected.
The hardest part is the first two weeks

CBT-I works, but it requires tolerating some short-term sleep deprivation to reset your sleep drive. Most patients see meaningful improvement by week 3–4. Do not give up in week one — that is when it feels worst and the treatment is actually working.

Understanding Insomnia

Insomnia is not one thing. Understanding your type helps us target treatment more effectively.

Types by symptom pattern

  • Sleep onset insomnia — difficulty falling asleep at the start of the night. Racing mind, anxiety, and hyperarousal are common drivers.
  • Sleep maintenance insomnia — difficulty staying asleep, with frequent or prolonged awakenings during the night. Often linked to aging, alcohol, pain, or mood disorders.
  • Early morning awakening — waking 1–2 hours before the desired time and being unable to return to sleep. Closely associated with depression.
  • Non-restorative sleep — sleeping a normal number of hours but waking unrefreshed. May suggest a sleep quality problem (e.g., sleep apnea, periodic limb movements) rather than true insomnia.

Acute vs. chronic insomnia

  • Acute insomnia — less than 3 months, often triggered by a specific stressor (new job, grief, illness, travel). Usually resolves on its own once the stressor passes. Short-term medication or sleep hygiene is usually sufficient.
  • Chronic insomnia — at least 3 nights per week for 3 or more months, causing daytime impairment. This is what CBT-I is designed to treat and what we evaluate and manage as a medical condition.

Common underlying drivers

  • Anxiety and worry — the most common trigger for sleep onset insomnia; a racing mind at bedtime is almost always anxiety-driven
  • Depression — especially early morning awakening; insomnia and depression maintain each other in a vicious cycle
  • ADHD — difficulty winding down, delayed sleep phase, and racing thoughts at night are extremely common in ADHD
  • Chronic pain — pain disrupts sleep architecture and creates frequent arousals
  • Caffeine and alcohol — both interfere with sleep quality even when they do not prevent sleep onset
  • Poor sleep hygiene — irregular schedule, screen use at night, and using bed for activities other than sleep all weaken the body's natural sleep cues
  • Medications — many common medications disrupt sleep (stimulants, SSRIs, beta-blockers, decongestants, steroids)
  • Sleep apnea — often misidentified as insomnia; snoring, gasping, or non-restorative sleep should prompt evaluation
Insomnia often has more than one cause

Most chronic insomnia involves a combination of factors — a predisposing personality or anxiety trait, a precipitating stressor that started it, and perpetuating behaviors (like extended time in bed or clock-watching) that keep it going long after the original trigger is gone. CBT-I directly targets the perpetuating factors, which is why it works even when the original trigger is no longer present.

Why Sleep Matters

Sleep is not passive downtime. It is when your brain consolidates memory, clears metabolic waste, and regulates hormones — and when your body repairs tissue, calibrates immune function, and restores energy. Chronic sleep deprivation has real, measurable consequences.

Physical health consequences

  • Cardiovascular — chronic insomnia significantly increases risk of hypertension, heart disease, and stroke
  • Metabolic — poor sleep impairs insulin sensitivity, raises cortisol, and promotes weight gain; increases risk of type 2 diabetes
  • Immune function — sleep-deprived individuals are 3x more likely to develop a cold when exposed to rhinovirus
  • Pain — poor sleep lowers your pain threshold, creating a cycle where pain disrupts sleep and poor sleep amplifies pain
  • Hormones — growth hormone, testosterone, and leptin are primarily secreted during sleep; disrupted sleep dysregulates all three

Mental and cognitive consequences

  • Mood — even one night of poor sleep amplifies emotional reactivity by up to 60% (amygdala hyperactivation)
  • Anxiety and depression — insomnia is both a symptom and a cause of anxiety and depression; they maintain each other bidirectionally
  • Memory and learning — sleep is when the brain transfers short-term to long-term memory; poor sleep undermines learning
  • Concentration and decision-making — sustained attention, working memory, and complex reasoning all degrade with sleep loss
  • Reaction time — 17 hours awake produces impairment equivalent to a blood alcohol level of 0.05%

Safety

  • Drowsy driving — microsleeps at the wheel account for tens of thousands of crashes annually; drowsy driving is as dangerous as drunk driving
  • Workplace accidents — higher rates of medical errors, industrial accidents, and occupational injuries in sleep-deprived workers
You cannot catch up on sleep debt

While a recovery night or two helps acute impairment, chronic sleep restriction is not fully reversible with weekend recovery sleep. The evidence is clear: there is no substitute for consistent, adequate nightly sleep. The good news is that effective insomnia treatment restores all of these functions relatively quickly — most patients notice mood, energy, and cognitive improvements within 2–4 weeks of improved sleep.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I)

CBT-I is the first-line recommended treatment for chronic insomnia — ahead of medication — according to the American College of Physicians, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, and virtually every major guideline. It works better than sleep medication in the long term and the benefits last after treatment ends.

Why CBT-I works better than medication long-term

Sleep medication reduces the symptom but does not change the underlying cause. CBT-I retrains your brain's relationship with sleep — rebuilding sleep pressure, breaking conditioned arousal in the bedroom, and restructuring the thoughts that keep you awake. Once those changes are made, they persist. Medication effects stop when you stop the medication.

The five components of CBT-I

1. Sleep restriction therapy

Temporarily compress your time in bed to match the amount you are actually sleeping. This builds sleep pressure (the drive to sleep), reduces time spent lying awake, and consolidates fragmented sleep. It is uncomfortable at first — you feel sleeper than usual during the day — but it is the most powerful single component of CBT-I. As sleep efficiency improves, time in bed is gradually extended.

2. Stimulus control

Rebuild the association between bed and sleep. The goal is for your brain to learn that bed = sleep, not bed = lying awake worrying. Key rules: use your bed only for sleep and sex; go to bed only when sleepy (not just tired); if you cannot sleep after ~20 minutes, get up and return only when sleepy again; get up at the same time every morning regardless of how you slept.

3. Sleep hygiene

Environmental and behavioral habits that support sleep. These alone rarely cure chronic insomnia but are essential support for the other components. See the Sleep Hygiene section for specifics.

4. Cognitive restructuring

Challenge and change unhelpful beliefs about sleep that perpetuate insomnia. Common examples: "I must get 8 hours or tomorrow will be ruined," "I haven't slept well in years and never will," "If I don't sleep tonight, something bad will happen." These beliefs increase arousal at bedtime and make sleep harder. CBT-I helps you examine the evidence and develop more accurate, less catastrophic thoughts about sleep.

5. Relaxation techniques

Lower physiological arousal at bedtime. Options include progressive muscle relaxation, diaphragmatic breathing, mindfulness meditation, and imagery-based relaxation. These are most helpful for sleep onset insomnia driven by anxiety and physical tension.

What to expect from CBT-I

  • Duration — typically 4–8 sessions with a therapist trained in CBT-I, or self-directed using a structured program
  • Week 1–2 — often harder than baseline; sleep restriction increases daytime fatigue. This is normal and expected.
  • Week 3–4 — sleep begins consolidating; most patients report meaningful improvement
  • Week 6–8 — most patients have achieved significantly improved sleep onset, fewer awakenings, and better morning function
  • Long-term — benefits persist and often continue to improve after treatment ends

Options for accessing CBT-I

  • Therapist-delivered CBT-I — the gold standard; ask us for a referral to a therapist trained in CBT-I in the Flower Mound / Keller area
  • Digital CBT-I (dCBT-I) — app-based and web-based programs deliver CBT-I with efficacy comparable to therapist-delivered treatment. Sleepio and Somryst (FDA-cleared) are evidence-based options.
  • Self-guided programs — books and workbooks can be effective for motivated patients. Say Good Night to Insomnia by Gregg Jacobs is the most evidence-backed self-help resource.
Medication + CBT-I together

For many patients, starting medication while beginning CBT-I is the right approach. Medication provides quick relief while CBT-I builds the foundation for lasting change. Once CBT-I is working, many patients are able to reduce or stop medication. We will work with you on a plan that makes sense for your situation.

Sleep Hygiene

Sleep hygiene is the foundation of every insomnia treatment plan. These habits will not cure chronic insomnia on their own, but ignoring them will undermine every other treatment you try. Think of them as non-negotiables.

Schedule

  • Set a fixed wake time and keep it every day — including weekends. This is the single most impactful habit change. Your circadian rhythm depends on consistency.
  • Go to bed only when sleepy — not just tired. If you are not sleepy, staying in bed trains your brain that bed is not for sleep.
  • Avoid naps longer than 20 minutes — or naps after 3 PM. Longer naps reduce sleep pressure and make it harder to fall asleep at night.

Environment

  • Keep your bedroom cool — 65–68°F is optimal for most adults. Core body temperature needs to drop to initiate sleep.
  • Make it dark — blackout curtains or a sleep mask. Any light source, including standby LEDs, can disrupt sleep architecture.
  • Keep it quiet — or use white noise / a fan to mask variable sounds. Sudden noise (not continuous noise) is the disruptive variable.
  • Use your bed only for sleep and sex — no phones, tablets, laptops, TV, or work. This sounds simple but is one of the most important stimulus control principles.

Light and screens

  • Avoid blue-light screens 30–60 minutes before bed — phones, tablets, and computers emit blue-wavelength light that suppresses melatonin and shifts your circadian clock forward
  • Get bright light exposure in the morning — ideally sunlight within 30–60 minutes of waking. Morning light anchors your circadian rhythm and improves sleep the following night.
  • Dim lights in the evening — starting 1–2 hours before bed, reducing light exposure signals the brain that night is coming

Substances

  • Avoid caffeine after 2 PM — caffeine's half-life is 5–7 hours; a 3 PM coffee still has half its caffeine in your system at 9 PM. Some people are slow metabolizers and need to cut off earlier.
  • Avoid alcohol within 3 hours of bedtime — alcohol helps you fall asleep faster but significantly disrupts sleep architecture. It suppresses REM sleep, increases awakenings in the second half of the night, and reduces total sleep quality even when total hours look the same.
  • Avoid large meals close to bedtime — digestion raises core body temperature and can cause discomfort. A small, light snack is fine if you are hungry.
  • Limit fluids 1–2 hours before bed — to minimize middle-of-the-night bathroom trips

Winding down

  • Build a pre-sleep routine — 20–30 minutes of the same calming activities each night trains your brain to associate those activities with approaching sleep
  • Write down tomorrow's to-do list before bed — "offloading" your mental checklist reduces the likelihood of lying awake running through it at 2 AM
  • Do not watch the clock — turn your clock away or cover it. Clock-watching increases anxiety and prolongs wakefulness.
  • If you cannot sleep after ~20 minutes, get up — go to another room, do something quiet and non-stimulating (reading on paper, light stretching, listening to calm audio), and return to bed only when you feel sleepy again

Exercise

  • Regular aerobic exercise significantly improves sleep quality — even moderate exercise like walking has measurable effects
  • Timing matters for some people — vigorous exercise within 1–2 hours of bedtime can delay sleep onset in some individuals (but not all). Morning or afternoon exercise is safest if you are sensitive.
  • Consistency matters more than intensity — 30 minutes of moderate exercise most days is more valuable for sleep than occasional intense workouts

Sleep Medications

Medication is an appropriate part of insomnia treatment — especially for acute insomnia, as a bridge during CBT-I, or when behavioral approaches have not been sufficient. At Buffington Family Medicine, we choose the right medication for your specific situation, sleep pattern, and comorbidities.

Medication is not a cure

Sleep medications treat the symptom. They do not fix the underlying cause of insomnia. For acute insomnia, this is often enough. For chronic insomnia, medication works best as a bridge to CBT-I or as an adjunct while behavioral changes take hold. We will discuss a plan for tapering when the time is right.

Non-benzodiazepine hypnotics (Z-drugs)

First-generation prescription sleep medications. Fast onset, generally well-tolerated, and effective for sleep onset. Tolerance and dependence risk with prolonged use.

Medication Duration Notes
Ambien / zolpidem IR 6–8 hours Most prescribed. Take immediately before bed. Available as generic.
Ambien CR / zolpidem ER 7–8 hours Extended-release; helps with both onset and maintenance insomnia.
Lunesta / eszopiclone 6–8 hours FDA-approved for long-term use. May cause metallic taste in some patients.
Sonata / zaleplon 4 hours Very short-acting. Can be taken in the middle of the night if ≥4 hours remain before wake time. Good for sleep onset only.

Orexin receptor antagonists (newer class)

Block the wake-promoting orexin signal rather than sedating broadly. Generally well-tolerated, low dependence potential, and effective for both sleep onset and maintenance insomnia. This class does not carry the same concern for tolerance and rebound as Z-drugs.

Medication Notes
Belsomra / suvorexant FDA-approved. Onset and maintenance. Available as generic (suvorexant).
Quviviq / daridorexant Newest in class (FDA 2022). Strong evidence for maintenance insomnia. Good next-day alertness profile.
Dayvigo / lemborexant FDA-approved 2019. Rapid onset, good maintenance. May cause next-day sedation at higher doses.

Off-label options (non-habit-forming)

Several medications approved for other indications have strong evidence for improving sleep and are commonly used when habit-forming medications are not appropriate or preferred.

Medication Primary Use Sleep Notes
Trazodone Antidepressant Low doses (25–100 mg) are sedating without the abuse potential of Z-drugs. One of the most commonly prescribed off-label sleep aids. Not a controlled substance.
Remeron / mirtazapine Antidepressant Sedating at low doses; especially useful when depression co-exists with insomnia. Often improves appetite as well.
Hydroxyzine (Vistaril) Antihistamine / anti-anxiety Prescription-strength antihistamine. Useful for anxiety-driven insomnia and short-term use. Not a controlled substance.
Melatonin Supplement Most useful for circadian rhythm disorders, jet lag, and shift work — not as effective for primary insomnia. Low doses (0.5–3 mg) taken 30–60 min before bed are preferred over the high-dose supplements commonly sold. Not regulated as a drug.

What we do not prescribe for insomnia

Medications to avoid for sleep

Benzodiazepines (Xanax, Klonopin, Ativan, Valium) are not recommended as sleep aids due to high tolerance development, physical dependence, rebound insomnia on discontinuation, next-day sedation, and cognitive impairment — especially in older adults. Benadryl / diphenhydramine (ZzzQuil, Unisom, PM pain relievers) should not be used for chronic insomnia — tolerance develops within days, and they cause anticholinergic effects including cognitive fog, urinary retention, dry mouth, and constipation. The cognitive effects are especially concerning in patients over 65.

What to Expect from Treatment

Starting medication

  • Night 1 — most sleep medications help the first night. You should notice faster sleep onset and/or fewer awakenings.
  • Week 1–2 — assess how you feel the next morning. Some next-day grogginess is normal early on and typically improves.
  • Week 2–4 — bring your experience to your follow-up. We will decide whether to continue, adjust the dose, or change the medication.

Starting CBT-I

  • Week 1–2 — expect to feel more tired than usual during the day. Sleep restriction builds sleep pressure — this is the mechanism working, not a sign of failure.
  • Week 3–4 — sleep begins consolidating. Most patients report fewer awakenings, faster sleep onset, or both.
  • Week 6–8 — most patients achieve significantly improved sleep with sustained results. Many report their best sleep in years.

What improvement looks like

  • Falling asleep within 20–30 minutes most nights
  • Waking fewer times during the night
  • Feeling more rested in the morning
  • Less dread or anxiety about going to bed
  • Improved mood and energy during the day
  • Better concentration and memory
  • Reduced reliance on caffeine to function
Perfect sleep is not the goal

The goal of insomnia treatment is not perfect sleep every night — that does not exist for anyone. The goal is sleep that is good enough, often enough that you are functioning well during the day and not preoccupied with sleep. Most successfully treated patients still have occasional poor nights; the difference is that those nights no longer derail them.

Side Effects & Management

Most side effects from sleep medications are mild and predictable. The most important ones to know about are below.

Side Effect Which Medications What to Do
Next-day drowsiness / grogginess All sleep medications, especially Z-drugs and mirtazapine Allow more time in bed initially. Do not drive until you know how you respond. Usually improves after 1–2 weeks as your body adjusts. If persistent, contact us — dose or timing may need adjustment.
Unusual sleep behaviors (sleepwalking, eating, driving while asleep) Z-drugs (zolpidem, eszopiclone) Stop the medication and call us immediately. These are rare but serious. More common if you take the medication and then stay awake or combine it with alcohol.
Metallic or bitter taste Lunesta / eszopiclone Common and harmless. Often improves over time. Drinking water before bed can help.
Rebound insomnia Z-drugs, benzodiazepines Temporary worsening of sleep when stopping the medication. Expected with prolonged use. Taper slowly under our guidance rather than stopping abruptly.
Next-day vivid dreams or grogginess Orexin antagonists (Belsomra, Quviviq, Dayvigo) Usually dose-related. Lower dose may help. Most patients find this improves over the first 1–2 weeks.
Dry mouth, morning grogginess Trazodone, hydroxyzine Common. Stay hydrated. Usually improves. If persistent, we may adjust the dose or timing.
Call us right away if you experience

Sleepwalking, sleep-driving, sleep-eating, or other unusual behaviors while on a sleep medication — stop the medication and call (817) 431-9199. Also call if you experience severe next-day sedation that prevents safe functioning, allergic reaction, or any thoughts of self-harm.

When We Refer for a Sleep Study

Not all sleep problems are insomnia. If your symptoms suggest a different sleep disorder — most commonly obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) — we will refer you for a sleep study rather than treating for insomnia alone.

Signs that suggest sleep apnea rather than insomnia

  • Loud snoring, especially witnessed pauses in breathing or gasping/choking sounds
  • Non-restorative sleep — sleeping 7–8 hours and still waking exhausted
  • Excessive daytime sleepiness — falling asleep in low-stimulation situations (meetings, driving, watching TV)
  • Morning headaches
  • Frequent nighttime urination (nocturia is commonly caused by OSA, not bladder issues)
  • High-risk profile — overweight, male, thick neck, large tonsils, retrognathia

Other conditions that may require referral

  • Restless leg syndrome (RLS) — uncomfortable crawling or aching sensations in the legs that are worse at rest and relieved by movement, worst in the evenings
  • Periodic limb movement disorder (PLMD) — involuntary leg movements during sleep, often reported by a partner rather than the patient
  • Parasomnias — unusual behaviors during sleep such as sleepwalking, sleep terrors, or REM sleep behavior disorder
  • Narcolepsy or hypersomnia — excessive sleepiness that is not explained by inadequate sleep time
Home sleep tests vs. in-lab studies

Most sleep apnea evaluations now use home sleep testing (HST) — a device you wear at home for 1–2 nights that measures breathing, oxygen levels, and heart rate. In-lab polysomnography is reserved for more complex cases or when HST results are inconclusive. We handle the referral and will explain what to expect from whichever study is ordered.

Keep a sleep log

A simple daily log — estimated time to fall asleep, number of awakenings, and how you felt in the morning — gives us far more useful information than memory alone. It does not need to be detailed: a few numbers each morning on your phone is enough. If you are using a smartwatch or sleep tracker, feel free to bring that data — though we focus primarily on how you feel, not what the device reports.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is insomnia dangerous?

Chronic insomnia has real health consequences — it increases risk of hypertension, heart disease, diabetes, depression, and accidents. It is not simply "bad nights." That said, insomnia itself is rarely acutely dangerous; the concern is the long-term cumulative effects of sleep deprivation on your health and safety.

Does alcohol help you sleep?

Alcohol helps you fall asleep faster but significantly disrupts sleep quality. It suppresses REM sleep in the first half of the night and causes rebound arousals in the second half as it metabolizes. The result is that you may sleep "through the night" but wake up unrefreshed. Regular alcohol use for sleep also builds tolerance quickly, meaning you need more to achieve the same effect — a pattern that worsens insomnia over time.

What about melatonin?

Melatonin is most effective for circadian rhythm problems — jet lag, shift work, or delayed sleep phase (being a "night owl" who cannot fall asleep until 2 AM). It has limited evidence for primary insomnia where the circadian clock is functioning normally. If you do use it, low doses (0.5–3 mg) taken 30–60 minutes before desired bedtime are preferred. Most over-the-counter melatonin is sold at doses (5–10 mg) far higher than what is effective — higher is not better.

Do I have to take sleep medication every night?

Not necessarily. Most sleep medications can be used on an as-needed basis — only on nights when you truly need help. Nightly use is sometimes appropriate for a limited period, but the goal for most patients is to use medication as infrequently as possible while building better sleep habits.

If I start sleep medication, do I have to take it forever?

No. The goal is to use medication for the shortest effective period. For acute insomnia, that might be 1–4 weeks. For chronic insomnia, medication may be used longer but is reassessed regularly with the goal of tapering as sleep habits improve through CBT-I. We will always have a plan for medication discontinuation, and we will help you taper safely when the time comes.

Can anxiety and depression cause insomnia?

Yes — and insomnia can cause and worsen anxiety and depression. This bidirectional relationship is one reason insomnia rarely resolves on its own when a mood disorder is present. Effectively treating anxiety or depression often improves sleep, and vice versa. We address both when they coexist.

What if I sleep 7–8 hours but still feel exhausted?

Non-restorative sleep — where you sleep adequate hours but wake unrefreshed — is often a sign of a sleep quality problem rather than insomnia. The most common cause is obstructive sleep apnea, which fragments sleep architecture even when total hours are normal. We will evaluate whether a sleep study is appropriate in this situation.

Is CBT-I covered by insurance?

Therapist-delivered CBT-I is typically covered under mental health benefits, similar to other therapy visits. Coverage varies by plan. Digital CBT-I programs like Sleepio are covered by some insurance plans and employers. Check your benefits or ask us for guidance. The out-of-pocket cost of a few therapy sessions is often worthwhile given CBT-I's long-term results.

Can I take sleep medication if I have sleep apnea?

Some sleep medications can suppress respiratory drive and worsen sleep apnea, particularly benzodiazepines and high-dose Z-drugs. Orexin receptor antagonists (Belsomra, Quviviq, Dayvigo) have a more favorable safety profile in sleep apnea. If you have known or suspected sleep apnea, tell us — it affects which sleep medications are appropriate for you.

I have ADHD and insomnia. Which do I treat first?

ADHD and insomnia are closely linked — racing thoughts at bedtime, difficulty winding down, and delayed sleep phase are hallmarks of ADHD. Treating ADHD effectively often improves sleep. However, ADHD stimulant medications can worsen sleep onset insomnia if timed incorrectly. We manage both simultaneously, optimizing stimulant timing and adding sleep-specific treatment when needed.

What is the difference between insomnia and just being a night owl?

Night owls have a delayed sleep phase — their circadian clock runs late, so they naturally fall asleep at 1–2 AM and wake at 9–10 AM. If allowed to sleep on their own schedule, they sleep fine. Insomnia involves difficulty sleeping regardless of when you go to bed. The distinction matters because treatment is different: delayed sleep phase responds well to strategic light exposure and melatonin; insomnia responds to CBT-I and medication.
